## part 2 – demystifying stem through personal projects 

the "black box" problem: explain that to most people, technology feels like magic inside a black box. STEM can feel exclusive or overwhelming when it lives purely in equations or code 

the power of building out in the open

when you take on a creative project, whether it's building an automated home system crafting custom hardware, or tinkering with software, you make the abstract concrete. 

personal projects take "scary tech" and make it visual, tangible , and fun.

storytelling moment (share a project story): 

example to practice: describe a project you worked on where something went completly wrong (a bug, a hardware failure, weather interfering).

focus on the struggle and the fix., not the code syntax. show them that even experienced builders run into brick walls, and that overcoming them is where the real learning happens. 

the takeaway: seeing someone build, fail, troubleshoot, and succeed in real time gives others permission to try it themselves. 

## part 3 why mentorship in stem matters now

connect personal action to the board community impact

turning "I Could never" into "show me how" 

the biggest barrier in STEM isn't aptitude; it's confidence 

mentorship bridges the gap between passive interest ("that looks hard" ) and active involvement ("I can do that too" ) 

the three pillars of stem mentorship 

lowering the barrier to entry: breaking down massive concepts into small, digestible steps

creating a safe place to fail: teaching that an error message or blow circuit isn't a dead end - it's just data.

building sustainable pipelines: supporting students and young professionals so they don't walk away when things get tough. 

community impact: mention how workshops, local projects, and collaborative technical initiatives spark long -term growth for students and professional chapters alike. 

### part 4  - how everyone can lead by example 

provide practical, actionable steps for the audience, regardless of their technical background. 


You don’t need a title to be a mentor: you don’t need “Senior directory” or “professor” in your title to help someone else grow

three ways to lead by example starting today: 

share your process, not just your result: don’t just show the finished, polished product. Show the messy working draft, the wire diagrams, or the rough draft. 

Invite someone in: the next time you work on a DIY fix, a creative endeavor, or a problem at work, bring someone along to watch or collaborate

encourage curiosity over perfection: praise the effort and the troubleshooting process rather than just the final success.
